If you live with arthritis, you know the cycle: the pain makes you want to move less, but moving less makes the pain worse. Joints stiffen. Muscles around the joint tighten protectively. Circulation slows. And the whole system gets locked into a pattern that feels impossible to break.

You've probably been told to "just keep moving" or "try some gentle stretching." But when your joints are inflamed and every movement feels like a gamble, generic advice doesn't help. What you need is a different approach—one that works _with_ your nervous system instead of against it.

That's where somatic movement comes in.

## Why Arthritis Creates a Tension Trap

Arthritis isn't just a joint problem. It's a whole-body experience. When a joint is painful, the muscles surrounding it instinctively tighten to protect the area. This is a natural, intelligent response—your body is trying to guard the vulnerable joint from further harm.

But when that protective tension becomes chronic, it creates a new problem. Tight muscles restrict circulation to the joint, reduce its range of motion, and increase pain signals sent to the brain. The joint gets less oxygen and fewer nutrients. Stiffness deepens. And the cycle continues.

"Somatic therapy and whole systems thinking doesn't reverse arthritis—but it does reduce inflammation, relieve tension, and help your joints move with better support," explains Dr. Melanie Carlone, who has over three decades of experience treating chronic joint conditions like osteoarthritis.[reference:0]

The key insight is this: the tightness around your arthritic joint isn't just a mechanical issue. It's a _nervous system_ issue. And that means it can be addressed through the nervous system.

## How Somatic Movement Differs from Stretching

Static stretching pulls on muscles that your brain has deliberately told to stay contracted. For arthritic joints, this can be counterproductive—pulling on tight, protective muscles may actually increase the guarding response and aggravate pain.

Somatic movement takes a completely different approach. Instead of forcing muscles to lengthen, it uses **pandiculation**—a voluntary contraction followed by a slow, conscious release. This three-step process (contract, slowly release, completely relax) engages the brain's motor control centers, retraining the nervous system to let go of unnecessary tension.

"Somatics is a movement practice that focuses on the internal perception of the body, emphasizing how it feels rather than how it looks," says Francesca Melluzzi, somatic movement therapist. "The primary purpose is to reconnect the brain to the body, undo habitual movement patterns, and promote a natural state."[reference:1]

For arthritis specifically, this matters because the tension around painful joints isn't just physical—it's neurological. The brain has learned to hold that tension as a protective strategy. Pandiculation speaks the brain's language.

## What the Research Says

While more research is needed specifically on somatic movement for arthritis, the evidence for gentle, mind-body movement is promising.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in _Frontiers in Public Health_ examined the effects of active mind-body movement therapies on older adults with osteoarthritis. The review found that these therapies—including Tai Chi, yoga, and qigong—had a small to moderate effect on pain relief, with Tai Chi being the most effective, followed by yoga and qigong.[reference:2] The analysis also found that exercise duration of 12 weeks or more produced better pain relief outcomes.[reference:3]

The Arthritis Foundation recommends Tai Chi as a safe and effective exercise for people with arthritis, noting that it improves strength, flexibility, and balance while reducing pain.[reference:4] Mindful movement practices like these share core principles with somatic movement: slow, controlled motion, attention to breath, and a focus on internal sensation rather than external performance.

More directly, research on mobilization with movement (a hands-on technique that shares principles with somatic work) has shown significant benefits for arthritis. A 2025 double-blind randomized controlled trial published in the _Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research_ found that mobilization with movement effectively increased joint space and reduced inflammation in the wrist and finger joints of rheumatoid arthritis patients. Inflammation was reduced by over 50% in the intervention group.[reference:5] While this study used a practitioner-applied technique, the underlying principle—gentle, mindful joint movement that restores normal glide—is central to somatic practice.

"Pain wants you to stop," says Dr. Jordan Sudberg, a pain management specialist. "But movement—done properly—isn't dangerous. It's healing. And it's often the most underused treatment for chronic pain."[reference:6]

Sudberg emphasizes that when you stop moving, your body doesn't get a break—it gets worse at healing. Reduced movement leads to muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, impaired circulation, and heightened pain perception.[reference:7]

## The 10-Minute Gentle Somatic Routine for Arthritis

This routine is designed for people with arthritis who want to reduce tension around painful joints, improve circulation, and restore a sense of control over their movement. It can be done seated in a chair, on a mat, or in bed.

**Important:** If you have acute inflammation, a recent joint replacement, or severe pain, consult your healthcare provider before starting any movement practice. Move within a pain-free range. The goal is _awareness_, not achievement.

### Before You Begin (1 minute)

Sit comfortably or lie on your back. Close your eyes if that feels safe. Take three slow breaths. Notice where you feel tension in your body. Don't try to change anything yet—just observe. This act of noticing is the foundation of somatic practice.

### Movement 1: Gentle Hand and Wrist Pandiculation (1 minute)

Arthritis commonly affects the hands and wrists. This movement brings circulation and awareness to these areas.

- Make a gentle fist with one hand. **Contract** the fingers and wrist slightly—about 30% of your maximum effort.
- Hold for 3 seconds, then **slowly release**, letting the fingers unfurl and the wrist soften.
- Repeat 3-4 times per hand. Notice any warmth or tingling as circulation returns.

### Movement 2: Shoulder Circles with Awareness (1 minute)

The shoulders often hold tension that refers down into the hands and up into the neck.

- Slowly roll your shoulders forward in small circles. **Contract** the shoulder muscles gently as you lift, then **slowly release** as you lower.
- After 5 circles forward, reverse direction. Keep the movement small and smooth.
- Notice if one shoulder moves more freely than the other. That difference is information, not failure.

### Movement 3: Pelvic Rock for Hip and Low Back Relief (2 minutes)

This gentle rocking motion releases tension in the hips and lower back—common sites of arthritic stiffness.

- Sit toward the front of a chair or lie on your back with knees bent.
- Slowly tilt your pelvis forward, then back, like a gentle rocking motion. **Contract** your lower abdominal muscles slightly as you tilt back; **slowly release** as you return.
- Do 8-10 slow rocks. Notice the changing pressure in your hips and low back.

### Movement 4: Knee Wandered In and Out (2 minutes)

For arthritic knees, gentle rotation and awareness can reduce protective tension.

- Seated or lying down, let one knee drift slowly inward toward the midline. **Contract** the inner thigh gently, then **slowly release**.
- Then let the knee drift outward. **Contract** the outer hip gently, then **slowly release**.
- Do 4-5 slow repetitions in each direction per leg. Keep the movement small—this is not a stretch.

### Movement 5: Full-Body Pandiculation (1 minute)

This is the integration movement—a voluntary, gentle yawn for the whole body.

- Reach both arms overhead and lengthen both legs away from you. **Contract** your whole body gently—about 30% effort. Yawn if you feel one coming.
- Hold for 3-5 seconds, then **release everything at once**, letting your arms and legs drop heavily.
- Rest completely for 10 seconds. Repeat 2-3 times. Notice the feeling of heaviness and softness.

### After the Routine (1 minute)

Sit or lie still for a minute. Scan your body from head to toe. Notice any areas that feel different—warmer, softer, lighter, or more even. This sensory feedback is your nervous system updating its map of your body. That's the change you're cultivating.

## What to Expect and What to Avoid

**What to expect:**

- A sense of warmth or tingling as circulation improves
- Reduced feeling of tightness around the joints
- Better awareness of where you hold tension
- Cumulative improvement over days and weeks of practice

**What to avoid:**

- Pushing through pain. If a movement hurts, make it smaller or skip it.
- Holding your breath. Keep breathing slowly and evenly.
- Judging your performance. This is not about doing it "right"—it's about feeling what's there.

"Staying in a really small range of motion is also a great way to start practicing these movements if you're in a lot of pain," advises Sarah Warren, a Certified Clinical Somatic Educator who has helped people with chronic back pain, hip and knee pain, and sciatica become pain-free.[reference:12]

## The Bigger Picture: Movement as Medicine

Thomas Hanna, the founder of Clinical Somatic Education, believed that aging and pain were not inevitable declines but patterns that could be changed. "As we grow older, our bodies—and our lives—should continue to improve, right up until the very end," he wrote.[reference:13]

That's a bold claim. But for people living with arthritis, the principle holds a powerful truth: the tension and stiffness around your joints is not a fixed sentence. It's a learned pattern. And what the nervous system has learned, it can unlearn.

Somatic movement isn't a cure for arthritis. It won't reverse joint damage or replace medical treatment. But it can change your relationship with your body—from one of guarding and fear to one of awareness and trust.

And that shift, over time, can change everything.
